Paper or Plastic?

A beginner's guide to environmental assessment, and an introduction to the field of industrial ecology.

Scroll to read :)

Paper or plastic? Ask a cashier, and they'll say paper. Ask a life cycle analyst, and they'll ask how many times you're going to reuse it.

Paper feels like the virtuous choice. It comes from trees, which grow back, and it breaks down instead of drifting through the ocean for decades. But making a paper bag takes more energy and chemicals than making a plastic one. Paper bags are also heavier, so every truckload moves fewer of them. A thin plastic bag, on the other hand, is made from fossil fuels and is rarely recycled: in the US, only about 10% of plastic bags, sacks and wraps were recycled in 2018. If one escapes into a river or the sea, it can take around 20 years to break down, crumbling into ever smaller pieces that harm wildlife along the way.

So which is better? It depends on what you care about. If your concern is climate change, the lightweight plastic bag often comes out ahead, unless you reuse the paper bag several times. If your concern is litter and ocean pollution, paper has the edge. It also depends on where you live. A city with good recycling, a clean electricity grid, and well-managed landfills faces a different trade-off than a coastal town where trash ends up on the beach.

There's no universal right answer, but there is a rigorous way to find the right answer for a given situation. That way of thinking has a name: industrial ecology.

The economy has a metabolism

Industrial ecology starts from a simple idea: an economy behaves like a living system. Materials and energy flow in, get transformed into products, and eventually flow back out as waste.

In a forest, there's no such thing as garbage. A fallen leaf becomes soil, and soil feeds the next tree. Most of our industrial system, by contrast, runs in a straight line: dig it up, make it, use it, throw it away.

Industrial ecologists ask what it would take to close that loop. The idea was popularized in 1989, when two General Motors researchers, Robert Frosch and Nicholas Gallopoulos, argued in Scientific American that factories could be designed to work more like ecosystems.

The classic real-world example is Kalundborg, a small industrial town in Denmark. Since the early 1970s, a power plant, a refinery, pharmaceutical and enzyme makers, and the town's utility have traded what would otherwise be waste. Steam from the power plant runs neighboring factories, and its leftover heat warms homes and businesses in town. In one of the first exchanges, a plasterboard maker dried its boards using gas the refinery had been burning off as waste. Nobody planned it as a grand experiment. Neighbors simply noticed that one company's trash was another's input.

Kalundborg shows what closing loops looks like. It is a perfect example of industrial symbiosis. But to know whether a loop actually helps the environment, you need to measure it. That's where the field's toolkit comes in.

Tool 1: the life cycle assessment

A life cycle assessment (LCA) is a biography of a product, from cradle to grave. It follows a grocery bag through four stages:

  1. Raw materials: drilling for oil, or growing trees or cotton
  2. Manufacturing: turning those materials into a bag, usually with electricity
  3. Use: carrying your groceries home, once or many times
  4. End of life: landfill, incineration, recycling, or a second life as a bin liner

At every stage, the analyst tallies what goes in (energy, water, land) and what comes out (greenhouse gases, pollutants, waste).

The first surprise is where the impact sits. The UK study found that for every bag type, making the material dominated the footprint: transport and disposal barely mattered. That means the question that matters most is how many trips you get out of what was made.

How many times do you need to reuse your bag?

The UK Environment Agency compared bags available in British supermarkets in 2006. It asked how many times each reusable bag must be used before its climate impact drops below that of a thin plastic bag used once.

That last point is worth pausing on. A thin plastic bag reused to line your trash can replaces a bin liner someone would otherwise buy. Both studies found that giving a light bag a second life this way beats recycling it.

The study also flagged an awkward fact about paper. Its authors noted the paper reuse number is hypothetical, because data from Ireland showed shoppers didn't actually reuse paper grocery bags. They tear, and they don't survive rain.

Same question, different country

Seven years later, the Danish Environmental Protection Agency ran its own study of bags in Danish supermarkets. It measured 15 kinds of environmental impact instead of focusing mainly on climate. It also asked how many extra uses each bag needs to match a sturdier plastic bag than the UK's reference.

On climate alone, unbleached paper came out on top in Denmark, the opposite of the UK result. Why? The Danish reference plastic bag was about three times heavier than the UK's thin one. The Danish bags were made in Europe, not shipped from Asia, and Danish waste is burned to generate energy rather than landfilled.

And those eye-popping cotton numbers? They come almost entirely from one impact category, ozone depletion, where the cotton production data looked especially bad. Leave that category out, and conventional cotton still needs somewhere between 50 and 1,400 uses depending on which impact you pick.

The lesson isn't that one study is wrong. It's that the answer depends on what you measure, what you compare against, and where you are.

Tool 2: following the flows

An LCA zooms in on one product. A material flow analysis (MFA) zooms out to a whole system: every bag in a country, or every tonne of copper in an economy.

Think of it as a bathtub. Flows pour in (new bags handed out at checkout), a stock sits in the tub (bags stuffed under your sink), and flows drain out (bags thrown away, recycled, or blown into a hedge). In 2008, UK shoppers were handed roughly 10 billion lightweight carrier bags, about 10 a week for every household. An MFA asks where all of them ended up. To study how these stocks and flows change over time, I think system dynamics is an excellent tool, and I plan to write a post on it soon, so keep an eye out.

This bigger picture changes the question. Instead of "which bag is best?", you can ask "if we ban one type, what replaces it, and how much of it?" A ban that swaps every thin plastic bag for a paper one that gets used once could raise the total footprint, even with the best intentions.

This is the kind of work I do in my own research, just with servers instead of shopping bags. Data centers hold a growing stock of copper, gold and rare earth metals. When servers are retired every few years, those metals flow out, and MFA helps us see how much could be recovered instead of lost.

How to read an environmental claim

You don't need to run an LCA to think like an industrial ecologist. The next time you see "50% lower emissions" or "eco-friendly" on a label, ask three questions.

1. Compared to what? Analysts call this the functional unit, the job being compared. The UK study compared bags carrying one month's shopping, 483 items. The Danish study compared one trip carrying 22 litres and 12 kilograms. A claim without a clear comparison is just a feeling.

2. Where did they draw the line? Every study has a system boundary: what's counted and what's left out. Here's the twist in our bag story. Neither study counted litter. The UK study left it out by design, and the Danish study judged it negligible for Denmark. The studies often cited to show plastic bags are "best" never measured the thing most people worry about.

3. Which impact? Carbon is not the only thing that matters. Water use, toxic chemicals, farmland, and ozone damage can all point in different directions. The cotton tote went from about 52 extra uses on climate to 7,100 across all impacts in the Danish study.

What the numbers can't tell you

These tools are powerful, but they're not oracles. Every number in an assessment is an estimate, and every estimate comes with uncertainty. Good data is often scarce, so analysts fill gaps with stand-ins. The UK study used European data for plastics actually made in Asia. The Danish study had to estimate how organic cotton is grown.

Uncertainty creeps in from several places: missing or outdated data, stand-in data from a different country or year, real-world variation (no two paper mills run the same way), and the modelling choices the analyst makes. None of this makes the results useless. It means a single number like "131 uses" is really the middle of a range.

The best studies say so openly. They test how much the answer moves when an assumption changes, which analysts call a sensitivity analysis. In the UK study, changing one assumption, whether people reuse thin plastic bags as bin liners, moved the cotton tote's break-even point from 131 uses to 327.

For me, this is where the real work of industrial ecology happens. Data quality isn't a footnote but it decides whether a result is something you can act on or just a confident-sounding guess. When I read an assessment, the first thing I look for isn't the headline number. It's how honest the authors are about what they don't know. This problem gets magnified many times over with electronics, where a single device holds dozens of materials and supply chains are hard to trace. That's a post of its own, coming soon, so stay tuned.

Place matters too. The UK's thin plastic bags were made in China, where coal-heavy electricity made up 38% of each bag's climate footprint. Make the same bag on a cleaner grid, and the answer shifts.

That's why two studies can disagree. The disagreement isn't a bug. It tells you which assumptions are doing the heavy lifting, and that's often the most useful thing an assessment can reveal.

So, paper or plastic?

Here's the answer an industrial ecologist would give. The best bag is the one you already own, used as many times as you can, and given a second life before it's thrown away.

Beyond that, it depends on what you care about. If climate is your priority, a thin plastic bag reused as a bin liner is hard to beat in many places. If litter and ocean plastic are your priority, a paper or durable reusable bag makes more sense, especially in a coastal town with patchy waste collection.

Places that ban plastic bags aren't necessarily wrong. They've decided that litter matters more to them than carbon. That's a values choice, and science can inform it but can't make it for us.

That's what industrial ecology offers: not a single verdict, but a way of seeing the whole system and asking better questions along the way.
