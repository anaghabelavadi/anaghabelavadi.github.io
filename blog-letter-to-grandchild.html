A Letter to My Grandchild

September 16, 2026 — Cambridge, Massachusetts

Dear grandchild,

I don't yet know what you'll call me, so forgive the generic opening.

I'm writing from my apartment in MIT graduate housing. The window in front of me looks out over the Charles River and the Boston skyline, and there are people running along the water. It's the first week of fall. The air I breathe is clean, the water I drink is clean, and I can step outside at any hour and walk. I know some of that is where I am. But a lot of it is when I am, and I'm not sure the world will offer you the same.

I should tell you who I am. Your parents have probably mentioned that I grew up in Bangalore, India. I went to good schools and had more than I needed, and it took me embarrassingly long to realize I was among the luckiest people in the country. When I moved to the US for graduate school, my life got even more comfortable. I got the best of both worlds. I don't know if that will be true for most people in yours.

Here is something I wish someone had explained to me as a child. Bangalore was famous for its mild weather. December mornings used to be cold. When I complained about the heat, my mother would say her winters were cooler, that the city wasn't what it used to be. I assumed she was exaggerating, the way parents do. She wasn't. The city's average temperature has been climbing by roughly a quarter of a degree every decade since the 1970s, and faster in recent years. I watched the summers get hotter with my own eyes and only understood what I was seeing years later. That delay is part of what I'm sorry for.

As I write, the planet has already had its first full year more than 1.5°C above pre-industrial levels. Three weeks ago, a glacier collapsed on the Nepal–Tibet border; the flood that followed has killed nearly 1,400 people and left about 5,000 missing. I read the news and don't know what to do with it.

I want to be honest with you about a smaller thing, too. I fly often between Massachusetts and California to see your grandfather. I'm vegetarian but eat a lot of dairy. I've stopped buying fast fashion. And every time I weigh giving something up, I catch myself thinking, other people do worse. I know that logic is exactly how a whole planet talks itself out of acting. Individual choices matter, but I've come to believe they can't carry this alone. It needs policy, infrastructure, and the systems underneath our lives to change.

Right now, I'm a PhD student doing sustainability research — the materials and energy inside the computing infrastructure the world is building at breathtaking speed — so that we build it, and rebuild it, with far less waste. It matters. But I still don't know how much one researcher can move, and I'm still learning.

Writing to you is clarifying something, though. Small lifestyle changes and one narrow research question aren't enough. I want to work at the level where the problem actually lives. I don't know yet what that looks like — whether it's the work I do, the ventures I help build, or the rooms I fight my way into — but I'm telling you now so that it counts as a promise: I will spend the rest of my life trying to make the world you grow up in better than the one I'm afraid of.

By 2075, I hope you take clean air, clean water, and a cold December for granted the way I once did. If we got there — if everything the scientific community was pushing toward actually worked — then I'm happier than I can say. If we didn't: I'm sorry. I'm sorry we didn't do better, and for my own small part in the world you inherited.

I'll be honest: I'm not feeling very optimistic tonight. But I've realized something while writing this. I want you to exist. I want to have a child and watch them have you, and I want everyone who comes after you to have what I've had. That is reason enough to keep going.

With love,
Your grandmother
